# Assumption Traps

Use this when classifying soft and untested constraints in Step 3. Each type has a typical sound, examples, and questions that expose it.

## The seven types

### 1. Historical: "We've always done it this way"
- Annual performance reviews (set by the calendar, not by how feedback works)
- An eight-hour training day (set by the work schedule, not by attention spans)
- A weekly status meeting that outlived the project it was created for

Ask: When did this start, and why? Has that reason changed? Starting fresh today, would we choose it?

### 2. Authority: "The experts (or the boss) say X"
- "Learners won't engage with self-paced material"
- "That engine can't handle a scene that size"
- "The customer will never approve that"

Ask: What evidence is behind this? What would prove it wrong? Is it a judgment or a measurement?

### 3. Analogy: "It's like Y, so the same rules apply"
- "Training is like school, so it needs lectures and tests"
- "A family schedule is like a work calendar"
- "This tool is like the last one we tried, so it'll fail the same way"

Ask: Are the underlying mechanics actually the same? Where does the comparison break?

### 4. Social: "Everyone does it" or "Nobody does that"
- "Every course has a final quiz"
- "The industry standard is X"
- "People would think it's weird"

Ask: Is this optimal or just common? Who benefits from it being the norm? What actually happens if we ignore it?

### 5. Resource: "We can't afford it" or "There's no time"
- "No budget for that" (often a priority choice rather than a hard limit)
- "We don't have anyone who can do it" (skills can be learned or borrowed)
- "I don't have time" (time is allocated; something else holds it)

Ask: Is this a real limit or a priority choice? What is the constraint behind the constraint? What would change if the resource appeared tomorrow?

### 6. Technical: "The technology can't do that"
- "The software doesn't support it"
- "The hardware is too slow"
- "It would take too long to compute"

Ask: Is this a physics limit or an engineering limit? What's the actual ceiling? Is there a workaround that moves the problem somewhere it's cheaper to solve?

### 7. Market or audience: "People want / don't want X"
- "Learners prefer video"
- "Nobody reads documentation"
- "Viewers won't watch anything over ten minutes"

Ask: Is this stated preference or observed behavior? Has it been tested in this context, with this audience?

## Phrases that signal a hidden assumption

| Phrase | What may be hiding |
|---|---|
| "Obviously..." | Something nobody has questioned |
| "Everyone knows..." | Consensus standing in for evidence |
| "It's impossible to..." | Difficulty mistaken for impossibility |
| "We have to..." / "It needs to..." | A convention presented as a requirement |
| "The problem is..." | A problem definition that may be wrong |
| "We tried that and..." | Conditions that may have changed since |
| "Best practice says..." | Best for whom, and when? |
| "I should..." / "I have to finish X before Y" | A self-imposed sequence or standard |

The last row matters for personal problems. Self-imposed rules about order, completeness, or quality are among the most common soft constraints and the least often examined.

## Stress test

For each assumption worth examining:

1. **State it** as a plain, testable claim.
2. **Trace its origin.** Where did the belief come from?
3. **Look for disconfirmation.** What would prove it wrong?
4. **Imagine the opposite.** What if the reverse were true?
5. **Find the cheapest test.** How could you check it in an hour or a day instead of a month?

## The outsider test

Imagine explaining the constraint to an intelligent outsider with no context about your field or organization. Would they accept "that's how it's done" as a reason? "The experts said so" without evidence? "It's too hard" without a concrete limit? If you can't justify it to them, it may be an assumption rather than a constraint.

## After finding an assumption

1. **Don't discard it immediately.** It exists for a reason.
2. **Understand why it became standard.**
3. **Check whether that reason still applies.**
4. **Weigh the cost of challenging it.** Some soft constraints are cheap to keep and expensive to fight, such as other people's expectations.
5. **Test it in small steps** rather than betting the whole plan on being right.

Most assumptions were once good solutions. Some still are.
